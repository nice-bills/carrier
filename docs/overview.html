Carrier

Dev3pack Global Hackathon · 30 Oct – 1 Nov 2026

A payment network that moves money without the internet. Value travels phone‑to‑phone, carried by strangers, and settles on Solana when anyone in the chain finds signal.

You do not join Carrier by connecting to an RPC. You catch it from someone standing near you. Everyone who carried a payment gets paid when it lands.

Why this doesn't already exist

Offline payments have been tried and abandoned, repeatedly

Two failures, every time. Carrier is a bet that both became solvable in the last twelve months.

Nobody relays for free

Carrying a stranger's data costs battery and earns nothing. So nobody does it, so the mesh has no carriers, so it dies at one node. Every mesh payment project has drowned here.

What changed Carriers are paid out of the note itself at settlement. Forwarding a stranger's payment is profitable. Per‑hop receipts only became affordable once compressed state made them cost roughly nothing.

Offline double‑spend is unsolvable

Strictly, it is. A device with no connection cannot check whether money was already spent. Most designs quietly pretend otherwise and lose trust the first time someone checks.

What we do instead We don't claim to have solved it. We bound the exposure to a number committed onchain in advance, detect cheating with a proof anyone can submit, and compensate the victim from a slashable bond.

The pieces

Three objects. One lives onchain, two live in people's pockets.

Pouchonchain

A pre‑funded allowance committed in advance, like a cashier's cheque book where the bank has already set the money aside. It carries a 256‑bit map of which cheque numbers have been cashed. Committed minus settled is the offline exposure ceiling — chosen by the user, visible to anyone.

Notein a pocket

One offline payment, signed by the sender, consuming one slot. It never touches the chain until it settles. The bitmap is what makes this work — an obvious monotonic counter would permanently strand every note that arrives out of order, which in a mesh is most of them.

Hopin a pocket

One device‑to‑device handoff, co‑signed by both phones. Not GPS — GPS is self‑reported and spoofable with commodity hardware. Two independent keys attesting to the same handoff is far harder to fake. The chain of hops is the transmission lineage. A chain runs to two hops — measured, not chosen: a third will not fit in one transaction.

One payment, end to end

What actually happens

  1. The sender goes offline and signs a note against an unused slot. No connection required, and none available.

  2. They hand it to whoever is nearby over Bluetooth. Both devices sign the handoff. That stranger now carries the payment.

  3. It keeps moving. Each new person met is another co-signed hop. The money travels by human movement, not by routing.

  4. Someone finds signal — possibly none of the people involved in the payment — and submits the whole accumulated bundle.

  5. Everything settles at once. The recipient is paid, every carrier in the chain takes a cut, and the lineage is written down.

The demo

Sixty seconds, no slides

Judges' phones in airplane mode. Send money across the room — there is no internet in the building. One judge walks to another; the payment hops. Still offline. The last one switches on wifi, and the entire chain settles at once, the lineage blooming on screen.

The submission rules cap demo videos at three minutes, strictly, and require the product running rather than a pitch deck. This demo needs about one, and it cannot be faked with a mockup — which is the point.

The hard part, stated plainly

What a cheat can actually do

A sender with a rooted device can sign two notes against the same slot and hand them to two different people. Both are valid. One settles; the other fails. Anyone who asks deserves this answer rather than a hand‑wave.

Bounded

Exposure can never exceed committed minus settled, across at most 256 notes per epoch. Both numbers are onchain and quotable before anything goes wrong.

Detected

Two conflicting notes are a proof. The program verifies both signatures itself — no oracle, no trusted reporter, no appeal to an operator.

Compensated

The bond is slashed. Whoever held the losing note is made whole from the cheat's own stake, and the person who brought the proof takes a cut.

On hardware with a secure element the slot map lives inside the enclave and the signing key never leaves it, so forging the second note means defeating the hardware rather than editing a file. That raises the cost of the attack. It does not eliminate it, and we don't say it does.

The end‑to‑end tests earned their keep: settlement did not fit in a Solana transaction at all. Five signatures carrying their messages inline came to 1557 bytes against a 1232‑byte limit, so a two‑hop payment could never have settled. Signing digests, sharing the blob both co‑signers cover, dropping data the program can derive, and moving account keys into a lookup table brought it to 1087. No unit test could have found that — none of them build a transaction.

Why this one, here

The field is not competing for this

Last edition's thirteen finalists were dominated by agent infrastructure — agent fund security took first, agent insurance took third, plus an agent firewall, an agent API layer, and an agent payments SDK. That lane is crowded and defended by teams with a four‑month head start.

Of roughly two dozen demo‑day teams, not one built anything physical, offline, or hardware‑bound. Meanwhile an inheritance protocol with a proof‑of‑life mechanism reached the finals out of Africa — evidence these judges reward ideas with human stakes rather than another chart.

And the build stations sit in Lagos, Enugu, Uyo, Lomé, Kampala, Lusaka, Caracas. Connectivity is not a hypothetical there. It is Tuesday.

Every other project in the room assumes an RPC connection. Half the people it's built for don't reliably have one.
